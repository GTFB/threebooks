# Road to Yourself

You know this feeling: you do everything "right" — and life answers *Access Denied*?

The same partner in a new package. The same wall in a new project. The same emptiness a month after "I'll change everything."

If something inside has been signaling that it's time — maybe it really is. Not to another tip. To root access.

---

Most people live with their psyche as guests.

They use what someone configured for them long ago. Press familiar buttons, walk familiar routes — and honestly don't understand why everything stalls. When they try to launch something big (solid relationships, serious income), the system coldly replies: *Insufficient permissions*.

Many inner processes aren't run by the person themselves. Background programs were written before birth. Maybe a great-grandfather's fear from being dispossessed in the thirties still blocks money growth — epigenetics passes the instruction "standing out is dangerous" to the body even when the mind lives in another century. Or a mother's old abandonment works as a filter cutting off closeness at the gate.

Affirmations and positive thinking won't fix this. Same as wiping a frozen screen with a cloth expecting the computer to work. The picture gets cleaner — the code stays.

```status
User: guest
Permissions: read-only
State: 42% capacity consumed by background anxiety
```

**ROOT. Road to Yourself** is about gaining full permissions in your own system.

Here you'll learn three things:

* **Read body signals.** Breath, tension, temperature, muscle tone — truth about you bypassing the mind.
* **Find bugs.** Typical failures in connections where energy drains to nowhere.
* **Apply patches.** Step by step: rewrite a broken rule, separate foreign from yours, reclaim energy.

When access opens, the body shifts mode: shoulders widen, breath drops into the belly. You don't have to drag yourself by force — clean code runs light.

---

## Who this book is for

Not for magic pills or abstract esoterica. For those ready to look under the hood and run an honest audit.

* **Stuck in "Insufficient permissions."** You sense you can do more — money, relationships, self-expression — but hit an invisible lock every time. Admin revoked your rights; you're a guest in your own life.
* **Processor overheated by background processes.** RAM goes to old resentments, debt, guilt, parent scripts, endless anxiety. You wake up drained because the system fought past conflicts all night.
* **Running foreign code.** You chase goals someone else wrote (parents, society, borrowed authority). Your firewall blocks asking what *you* want.
* **Hardware reporting critical errors.** Jaw locked, cold fingers, shallow breath, lump in throat. Mind says "fine" while the body screams overload — time to debug.

A guide for life engineers ready to admit old scripts no longer work. Time to enter ROOT mode.

## Zhora and Kristina

Zhora wrote code like others breathe — automatic, quiet. Eight years in IT forged a machine that found bugs in seconds. People — never understood.

Kristina entered his life like a syntax error in clean code: sudden, irritating, inexplicably magnetic. She laughed too loud — and Zhora didn't want her to stop.

Then hell. She said he "doesn't hear" her. Zhora honestly didn't get it: he heard every word, could repeat them like a log file. But Kristina cried while he stood blank-faced with cold, shapeless panic inside.

— You're like a robot. Do you feel anything at all?

That night Zhora opened a browser and searched — methodically, with documentation. Found ROOT. Bought it. Started reading at 3 a.m.

*Your reactions today are echoes of someone else's pain yesterday.*

He reread the line four times. Understood why he freezes when Kristina is angry: no male anger at home as a child — only female exhaustion and resentment toward a father who once erased his son — not with rage, like closing a project. Dad started a new family. Zhora stayed with two women who taught him to be strong, not cry, not ask.

He tried. Learned to name states aloud, ask "how do you feel" without answering with a fix. Sometimes it worked.

But trauma isn't a bug fixed by a patch. It's architecture.

Kristina left gradually — signal weakening bar by bar. Zhora saw it. Now knew how to see. That was unbearable.

The last talk was quiet. No shouting.

— You became different. Truly. But I'm tired. I can't wait until you grow.

Zhora wanted to say many things. Said nothing. She was right.

Evening alone in the apartment that still smelled of her perfume. Inside something huge — not anger, not resentment. Just pain. Real, without irony or analysis as armor.

Zhora didn't drink. Didn't call. He picked up the book and opened the next chapter.

Some things you realize too late. But continuing is also root access — not when the light returns, but when only you remain, an empty room, and honesty without excuses.
